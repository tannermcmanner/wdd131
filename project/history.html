History

How Parkour Began

Parkour traces its roots to 1902, when French naval officer Georges Hébert developed the "Méthode Naturelle," a training system built on natural movements like running, jumping, climbing, and balancing. That philosophy later inspired Raymond Belle, whose son David Belle grew up practicing those same ideas. In the late 1980s, David began adapting them to the urban landscape of Lisses, France, and with a group of friends he helped shape what would become parkour.

The Word "Parkour"

In 1997, David Belle and fellow practitioner Hubert Koundé settled on a name for the discipline. Koundé adapted "parcours," the French word for "course" or "route," from Raymond Belle's obstacle-course training. Dropping the silent "c" and swapping it for a "k" gave the sport its name: parkour.

Red Bull Art of Motion

In 2007, Red Bull launched Art of Motion in Austria, a competition built around creativity, flow, and style. It quickly became the biggest stage in the sport, drawing top athletes from around the world and bringing parkour to a massive new audience. After years of influence on the community, the event came to an end in 2022.

The Evolution of Parkour

Over the years, parkour has had several breakoffs, each taking the movement in its own direction. Here are a few examples.

Freerunning

Freerunning began forming in the early 2000s. Parkour focuses on efficient movement from point A to point B, while freerunning emphasizes flips and tricks that add flair. Today the two terms are largely synonymous, as the two facets have mostly merged into one.

Tricking

Tricking emerged in the early 2000s, blending martial arts, gymnastics, and breakdancing. It is mostly a connected line of flips, kicks, and twists performed on flat ground.

G Tramp

G Tramp, short for "garden trampoline," started taking shape in the 2010s on classic backyard trampolines. The sport focuses on big, high-flying trampoline tricks.

Wall Tramp

Wall Tramp grew out of G Tramp in the 2010s. A wall is positioned right next to the trampoline, letting the jumper use it to bounce off and perform tricks that are not possible on the trampoline alone.
